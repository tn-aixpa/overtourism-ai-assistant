# Workflow for the Methodological Design of Territorial Indices

## Purpose

This workflow supports the definition, design, and methodological
assessment of indices that describe tourism, mobility, sustainability,
and related territorial phenomena at municipality level.

Its goal is to guide the creation of **new territorial indices**: from
the identification of the phenomenon to be measured, through the
selection and preparation of the underlying variables, to the
definition, interpretation, and validation of the final index.

The workflow is methodological rather than technical. It should explain
**what an indicator measures, why it is relevant, what evidence is
needed, and how it should be constructed and interpreted**. Technical
implementation, data engineering, and software-specific decisions are
outside the scope of this document.

A territorial index normally produces a comparable value for each
municipality in the territory. It may subsequently be used to produce
maps, comparisons, time series, territorial profiles, or higher-level
territorial aggregates.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 1. Define the Territorial Phenomenon

The first step is to clearly define the phenomenon that the new index is
intended to measure.

The phenomenon should be expressed in terms that are meaningful for
territorial planning, tourism management, sustainability, or the
relevant policy domain.

Examples include:

-   tourism intensity;
-   accommodation capacity;
-   concentration of tourism;
-   dependence on tourism;
-   pressure generated by visitors;
-   seasonality;
-   accessibility;
-   mobility intensity;
-   use of tourism infrastructure;
-   distribution of tourism benefits;
-   environmental pressure;
-   social or cultural impacts;
-   resilience of a destination.

The definition should answer:

-   **What phenomenon is being measured?**
-   **Why is it relevant for the territory?**
-   **What territorial question should the index help answer?**
-   **Who would use the information?**
-   **At what spatial scale should it be meaningful?**
-   **Over what period should it be measured?**

An index should not be created simply because data are available. The
methodological need should come first, followed by the identification of
appropriate data.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Establish the Conceptual Framework

Before choosing variables, define the conceptual dimension that the
index represents.

A useful index should have a clear conceptual relationship between:

**territorial phenomenon → measurable dimensions → observable variables
→ indicator**

For example, a broad concept such as tourism pressure may involve
several dimensions:

-   intensity of visitors relative to residents;
-   concentration in space;
-   concentration in time;
-   pressure on accommodation capacity;
-   pressure on mobility infrastructure;
-   pressure on environmental resources.

These dimensions should not automatically be collapsed into one number.
First determine whether they represent:

-   different indicators of the same phenomenon;
-   complementary indicators that should remain separate;
-   or components that can legitimately be combined into a composite
    index.

The conceptual framework should make clear what the index captures **and
what it does not capture**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. Use Established Sustainability and Tourism Frameworks

When an index concerns sustainable tourism or territorial
sustainability, its conceptual design can be guided by established
international frameworks.

Two important reference points are:

## Global Sustainable Tourism Council (GSTC)

The **GSTC Destination Criteria** provide a framework for sustainable
destination management covering, among other areas, sustainable
management, socio-economic sustainability, cultural sustainability, and
environmental sustainability.

The criteria emphasize monitoring and reporting of tourism-related
environmental, economic, social, cultural, and other impacts, as well as
issues such as tourism seasonality, the economic contribution of
tourism, local employment and benefits, cultural heritage, resource use,
and environmental impacts. citeturn0search13turn0search14

GSTC can therefore be used as a **conceptual reference when deciding
which dimensions of destination sustainability should be represented by
a set of territorial indicators**.

It should not be assumed that every GSTC criterion must become a
numerical index. The criteria can instead be used to identify relevant
dimensions, gaps, and possible measurement needs.

## UN Tourism

**UN Tourism** provides international guidance and initiatives
concerning sustainable, inclusive, and resilient tourism and its
contribution to territorial development.

Its work includes approaches that consider economic, social,
environmental, cultural, governance, infrastructure, connectivity, and
community dimensions. For example, the UN Tourism *Best Tourism
Villages* framework evaluates destinations across areas including
cultural and natural resources, economic sustainability, social
sustainability, environmental sustainability, tourism development and
value-chain integration, governance, infrastructure and connectivity,
and health, safety and security. citeturn0search0turn0search3

UN Tourism frameworks can therefore be used to:

-   identify relevant dimensions for territorial measurement;
-   structure groups of indicators;
-   check whether important dimensions are missing;
-   connect territorial indicators with broader sustainable-development
    objectives.

These frameworks are **methodological references**, not mandatory
formulas. A new index should still be justified according to the
specific territorial question it is intended to answer.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. Define the Unit of Analysis

Territorial indices in this context are calculated at **municipality
level**.

The municipality should therefore be the fundamental unit to which the
final index refers.

This requires deciding:

-   which municipalities are included;
-   how municipalities are identified;
-   whether the same territorial boundaries are used across all data
    sources;
-   whether historical territorial changes affect comparability;
-   whether the index is intended to describe the municipality itself or
    a wider functional territory.

The distinction between the **unit of measurement** and the
**territorial interpretation** is important.

For example, an index may be calculated for every municipality but later
interpreted at:

-   municipality level;
-   tourism district level;
-   province level;
-   regional level.

The aggregation or grouping of municipalities should not change the
conceptual definition of the underlying municipality-level index unless
a separate methodological aggregation is explicitly defined.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5. Identify the Dimensions and Variables

Once the phenomenon and conceptual framework are defined, identify the
variables required to measure it.

For each variable, document:

-   what it measures;
-   why it is relevant;
-   its source;
-   its spatial coverage;
-   its temporal coverage;
-   its unit of measurement;
-   its frequency;
-   its expected quality and limitations;
-   whether it is directly observed or estimated.

Typical variables may include:

-   resident population;
-   accommodation beds;
-   accommodation establishments;
-   tourist arrivals;
-   overnight stays;
-   mobile-network estimated presences;
-   mobility flows;
-   transport capacity;
-   employment;
-   economic activity;
-   environmental measurements;
-   infrastructure capacity.

A variable should be included because it contributes to the conceptual
definition of the index, not merely because it is easy to obtain.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Determine the Temporal Reference

A territorial index must have a clearly defined temporal reference.

The methodology should specify whether the index describes:

-   a single day;
-   a month;
-   a season;
-   a year;
-   an average over a period;
-   a cumulative quantity;
-   a change between two periods.

Different variables may initially be available at different temporal
resolutions.

For example:

-   population may be available annually;
-   accommodation capacity may be available annually;
-   tourist presences may be available daily;
-   mobility measurements may be available daily or at another
    frequency.

These differences must be resolved methodologically before the variables
are combined.

The chosen temporal treatment should be documented and justified.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Align Variables to a Common Spatial and Temporal Basis

Variables used in the same index must be comparable.

Before combining them, establish a common:

-   spatial unit;
-   temporal reference;
-   definition;
-   measurement basis.

When a source is available at a coarser spatial level, such as a
province or region, a method may be required to estimate
municipality-level values.

Similarly, when a source is available monthly or yearly but the index
requires a finer temporal resolution, an appropriate temporal allocation
may be necessary.

Possible approaches include:

-   direct use when the source already matches the target resolution;
-   carrying a value across the relevant period when the value is
    assumed constant;
-   distributing a coarser value using an appropriate reference
    variable;
-   performing the disaggregation during data preparation.

Any estimated allocation or disaggregation introduces assumptions and
must therefore be documented.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. Choose the Mathematical Form of the Indicator

After defining the conceptual variables, decide how they should be
combined.

The mathematical form should correspond directly to the phenomenon being
measured.

Common forms include:

### Ratios

A ratio relates two quantities with a meaningful denominator.

Examples:

``` text
accommodation capacity
----------------------
resident population
```

or:

``` text
tourist presence
----------------
resident population
```

Ratios are useful when the absolute value of a phenomenon needs to be
related to the size or capacity of the territory.

### Shares or proportions

A share measures the contribution of one category to a total.

For example:

``` text
non-conventional facilities
---------------------------
total facilities
```

or:

``` text
non-conventional beds
---------------------
total beds
```

### Rates of change

A rate of change compares the same indicator across two periods.

Conceptually:

``` text
current value - reference value
------------------------------- × 100
       reference value
```

This is useful for measuring growth or decline rather than absolute
intensity.

### Period incidence

An index may measure the share of a selected sub-period relative to the
total period.

For example:

``` text
value during selected period
---------------------------- × 100
value during total period
```

This can be used to describe seasonality or concentration in a specific
period.

### Composite indices

Several indicators may be combined into a higher-level index when they
represent complementary dimensions of the same conceptual construct.

Composite indices require additional methodological decisions
concerning:

-   normalization;
-   weighting;
-   aggregation;
-   treatment of missing values;
-   sensitivity to individual components.

A composite index should only be used when the conceptual justification
for combining its components is clear.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Define the Denominator Carefully

For ratio-based indicators, the denominator is a methodological choice
and should receive particular attention.

The denominator determines what the numerator is being measured
**relative to**.

For example, tourism presence can be expressed relative to:

-   resident population;
-   accommodation capacity;
-   territorial area;
-   another relevant capacity measure.

These alternatives answer different questions and should not be treated
as interchangeable.

The choice should be justified according to the intended interpretation
of the index.

A ratio should also be checked for cases where the denominator is zero,
missing, unstable, or not conceptually appropriate.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 10. Define the Interpretation and Unit

Every new index should have an explicit interpretation.

The methodology should state:

-   what a high value means;
-   what a low value means;
-   whether the value is a ratio, percentage, count, rate, or other
    measure;
-   the unit in which it is expressed;
-   whether the value has an intuitive reference point.

For example:

**Indice di ricettività** measures the ratio between accommodation beds
and resident population. Its interpretation is therefore the
accommodation capacity relative to the resident population.

**Indice di turisticità** measures the ratio between average daily
tourist presence and resident population. Its interpretation concerns
the incidence of tourism relative to the resident population.

The interpretation must follow the formula. A numerical value should not
be assigned a qualitative meaning unless that meaning has been
methodologically established.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 11. Distinguish Measurement from Evaluation

An index should first be designed as a **measurement**.

For example, an index can measure:

-   tourism intensity;
-   accommodation capacity;
-   seasonality;
-   environmental pressure;
-   concentration.

The index itself does not automatically establish whether a value is
desirable or undesirable.

If thresholds, target values, warning levels, or classifications are
introduced, they require a separate methodological justification.

This distinction is particularly important for sustainability
indicators: a high or low value may have different implications
depending on the phenomenon and the territorial context.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 12. Consider Comparability Between Municipalities

A territorial index is usually intended to support comparison between
municipalities, so comparability must be considered explicitly.

Before comparing values, verify:

-   that the same definition is used for every municipality;
-   that the same period is used;
-   that the same source definitions are used;
-   that the same spatial boundaries are used;
-   that the same transformation and disaggregation rules are applied;
-   that differences in data quality do not create artificial
    differences.

A municipality with a higher value is not necessarily experiencing a
greater impact or a worse situation. The interpretation depends on what
the indicator measures.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Consider Data Quality and Uncertainty

The methodology should explicitly document the main limitations of the
underlying data.

Consider:

-   measurement error;
-   missing data;
-   changes in source methodology;
-   differences in source coverage;
-   estimates and proxies;
-   spatial disaggregation;
-   temporal disaggregation;
-   changes in territorial boundaries;
-   small denominators;
-   inconsistent definitions across years;
-   uncertainty associated with indirect measurements.

Where a variable is estimated rather than directly observed, this should
be made explicit.

In particular, mobile-network data and other indirect measurements may
provide valuable information about territorial presence or mobility, but
they should be distinguished from administrative or officially recorded
tourism statistics.

The objective is not to eliminate uncertainty, but to make the
assumptions and limitations transparent.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Check Whether the Index Adds Information

Before creating a new index, verify whether the proposed measure
provides information that is not already adequately captured by existing
indicators.

Ask:

-   Does it measure a distinct phenomenon?
-   Does it answer a relevant territorial question?
-   Does it provide information that existing indicators do not provide?
-   Is the mathematical transformation meaningful?
-   Is the additional complexity justified?

A new index should not simply reproduce an existing measure under a
different name.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 15. Consider Whether a Single Index Is Appropriate

Not every territorial phenomenon should be reduced to one number.

If a phenomenon has several dimensions that cannot be meaningfully
combined, it may be preferable to define a **set of complementary
indicators** rather than a single composite index.

For example, sustainable tourism may involve economic, social, cultural,
environmental, governance, and infrastructure dimensions. International
frameworks such as GSTC and UN Tourism explicitly address multiple
dimensions rather than assuming that sustainability can always be
represented by one number. citeturn0search13turn0search0

A methodological design should therefore distinguish between:

-   a **single indicator** measuring one well-defined relationship;
-   a **family of indicators** describing different dimensions;
-   a **composite index** combining multiple indicators into one
    measure.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 16. Validate the Proposed Index

Before adopting a new index, validate it conceptually and empirically.

### Conceptual validation

Check whether:

-   the formula corresponds to the stated definition;
-   the selected variables actually represent the phenomenon;
-   the denominator is appropriate;
-   the expected direction of interpretation is clear;
-   the index does not combine incompatible concepts.

### Data validation

Check whether:

-   the necessary data exist for all municipalities;
-   the temporal coverage is sufficient;
-   the spatial coverage is sufficient;
-   the variables are sufficiently reliable;
-   missing data are manageable;
-   estimated values are identified.

### Sensitivity validation

Where relevant, test whether reasonable changes in:

-   weights;
-   normalization;
-   reference periods;
-   disaggregation assumptions;
-   component variables

substantially change the resulting territorial picture.

If the result is highly sensitive to an assumption, that sensitivity
should be documented.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 17. Document the Indicator

Every new territorial index should have a methodological definition that
can be understood independently of its technical implementation.

At minimum, document:

-   **Name**
-   **Purpose**
-   **Phenomenon measured**
-   **Conceptual dimension**
-   **Unit of analysis**
-   **Temporal reference**
-   **Input variables**
-   **Definition of each variable**
-   **Data sources**
-   **Formula**
-   **Unit of measurement**
-   **Interpretation**
-   **Expected direction, where meaningful**
-   **Data-quality limitations**
-   **Disaggregation or estimation assumptions**
-   **Valid time period**
-   **Relevant sustainability or tourism framework**
-   **Known limitations**
-   **Validation approach**

The documentation should be sufficiently precise that another analyst
can understand what the indicator means without reading the
implementation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 18. Overall Procedure for Designing a New Territorial Index

The complete methodological workflow can be summarised as:

1.  **Identify the territorial question.**
2.  **Define the phenomenon to be measured.**
3.  **Identify the conceptual dimensions involved.**
4.  **Consult relevant established frameworks**, where appropriate, such
    as GSTC and UN Tourism.
5.  **Define the municipality as the unit of analysis**, or explicitly
    justify another spatial unit.
6.  **Identify the variables needed to measure the phenomenon.**
7.  **Assess the quality, coverage, and limitations of the available
    data.**
8.  **Define the temporal reference.**
9.  **Align spatial and temporal definitions across variables.**
10. **Define any required estimation or disaggregation assumptions.**
11. **Choose the mathematical form of the indicator.**
12. **Define the denominator and aggregation logic where applicable.**
13. **Define the unit and interpretation of the resulting value.**
14. **Check comparability between municipalities and over time.**
15. **Validate the conceptual and empirical behaviour of the index.**
16. **Assess whether a single index, a family of indicators, or a
    composite index is most appropriate.**
17. **Document the methodology, assumptions, limitations, and sources.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Core Principle

A territorial index should be designed starting from the **question that
the territory needs to answer**, not from the data that happen to be
available.

The methodological chain is:

``` text
Territorial question
        ↓
Phenomenon to measure
        ↓
Conceptual dimensions
        ↓
Observable variables
        ↓
Data sources
        ↓
Spatial + temporal alignment
        ↓
Methodological assumptions
        ↓
Mathematical definition
        ↓
Territorial index
        ↓
Interpretation + validation
```

The index is therefore not simply a formula applied to available data.
It is a methodological construct that should have a clear purpose,
conceptual basis, transparent assumptions, appropriate data, and an
interpretation that remains faithful to what is actually being measured.
